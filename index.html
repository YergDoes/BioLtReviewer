1 Mendel's Approach & Core Vocabulary

🎯 Learning Targets
  • Explain Mendel's contribution to genetics.
  • Distinguish genes, alleles, genotype, and phenotype.
  • Differentiate homozygous and heterozygous conditions.

Why Are We Talking About Coins?

An event is random when the outcome of a previous event does not affect the outcome of the succeeding event. A coin toss is the classic example: if the first toss is heads, the second toss is still 50% heads and 50% tails.

Mendelian inheritance involves random events + probability. A heterozygous pea plant (Gg) produces:

  • G gametes → 1/2 of the time
  • g gametes → 1/2 of the time

When two gametes unite, probability helps us predict the possible genotype of the offspring.

🪙 Coin Toss Examples
  • P(Head AND Head) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 (25%)
  • P(one head AND one tail) = (1/2 × 1/2) + (1/2 × 1/2) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2 (50%)

Gregor Johann Mendel — Father of Modern Genetics

Studied inheritance using pea plants (Pisum sativum). His experiments investigated:

  • What is inherited?
  • How is hereditary information inherited?
  • Why do offspring show different traits?
  • What role does probability play in heredity?

Why Pea Plants?

✅ Advantages
  • Easy to grow
  • Produced many offspring
  • Relatively short generation time
  • Had many contrasting traits
  • Could self-fertilize
  • Could be cross-pollinated in a controlled manner

Generations in a Genetic Cross

P GENERATION (Original Parents) Purple × White ↓ F₁ GENERATION (First Offspring) ALL PURPLE ↓ F₁ × F₁ ↓ F₂ GENERATION (Offspring of F₁) 3 PURPLE : 1 WHITE

Where did the white trait go in the F₁ generation? It was masked, not destroyed. It reappeared in the F₂ generation.

Mendel's "Factors" → Genes & Alleles

Mendel proposed discrete hereditary units called factors. Today, we call them GENES.

A gene can have alternative forms called ALLELES.

🔬 Example — Flower Color
  • Gene: flower color
  • Alleles: P = purple, p = white
  • An individual receives one allele from each parent.

Genotype vs. Phenotype

🧬 Genotype

The combination of alleles possessed by an organism.

Examples: PP, Pp, pp

👁️ Phenotype

The observable characteristic of an organism.

Examples: Purple flowers, White flowers

2 Mendel's Laws & Probability

🎯 Learning Targets
  • Explain the Law of Segregation and Law of Independent Assortment.
  • Apply the multiplication and addition rules of probability.

Law of Segregation (Mendel's First Law)

📜 Definition
The two alleles for a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one allele.

Example: Gg → G OR g (50% each).

Meiotic basis: Connected to the separation of homologous chromosomes during Meiosis I.

🔬 Seed Color Example
  • P: YY (yellow) × yy (green)
  • Each homozygous parent forms only 1 kind of gamete: Y or y
  • F₁: all Yy (heterozygous)
  • The Yy offspring forms 2 kinds of gametes: Y and y
  • F₂: self-pollination gives a 3:1 phenotypic ratio (yellow : green)

Law of Independent Assortment

📜 Definition
Two or more genes assort independently — each pair of alleles segregates independently of the other pair during gamete formation.

Example: YyRr can produce four types of gametes: YR, Yr, yR, yr.

This demonstrates that different combinations of alleles produce genetic variation.

Probability Rules

Multiplication Rule ("AND")

When two or more independent events must occur together, multiply their probabilities.

Formula: P(A AND B) = P(A) × P(B)

Example: P(gg from Gg × Gg) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4

Addition Rule ("OR")

When either of two mutually exclusive events can occur, add their probabilities.

Formula: P(A OR B) = P(A) + P(B)

Example: P(Gg from Gg × Gg) = (1/4) + (1/4) = 1/2

🧠 Key Idea

AND → MULTIPLY  |  OR → ADD

The Testcross

Problem: A plant is tall, but we don't know if its genotype is TT or Tt.

Solution: Cross it with a homozygous recessive individual (tt).

Case 1: TT × tt

All offspring tall → Unknown parent is TT.

Case 2: Tt × tt

1:1 ratio (50% tall, 50% short) → Unknown parent is Tt.

🔑 Key Idea
A recessive phenotype in the offspring reveals that the unknown parent contributed a recessive allele.

4 Dihybrid Crosses & Forked-Line Method

🎯 Learning Target
  • Apply probability to solve dihybrid and multi-hybrid crosses using the forked-line method.

Dihybrid Cross: GgWw × GgWw

A 4 × 4 Punnett square contains 16 boxes. But probability rules can make the problem much faster.

Gene 1: Seed Color
  • Gg × Gg
  • Green = 3/4
  • Yellow = 1/4
Gene 2: Seed Shape
  • Ww × Ww
  • Round = 3/4
  • Wrinkled = 1/4

Probability of Green AND Round: 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16

The Forked-Line Method

Applies probability to each gene separately, then multiplies along branches.

Gene 1 Gene 2 Phenotype & Probability ┌─ 3/4 round ──→ 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16 green, round 3/4 green ──────────┤ └─ 1/4 wrinkled → 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16 green, wrinkled ┌─ 3/4 round ──→ 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16 yellow, round 1/4 yellow ─────────┤ └─ 1/4 wrinkled → 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16 yellow, wrinkled

Phenotypic ratio: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Trihybrid Example — AaBbCc × AaBbCc

Instead of a 64-box Punnett square, multiply the probabilities for each gene:

Dominant for all three (A_B_C_)3/4 × 3/4 × 3/4 = 27/64
Dominant for two, recessive for one3/4 × 3/4 × 1/4 = 9/64 (each)
Dominant for one, recessive for two3/4 × 1/4 × 1/4 = 3/64 (each)

6 Multiple Allelism

🎯 Learning Targets
  • Describe multiple allelism and identify its distinctive features.
  • Identify the genotypes and phenotypes of the ABO blood groups.
  • Predict possible offspring genotypes and phenotypes.
  • Solve problems involving multiple alleles.
❓ Guiding Question
Can a single gene have more than two possible alleles? Yes.

What is Multiple Allelism?

A character is under multiple allelism when a gene has more than two possible alleles within a population.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Multiple allelism does NOT mean one individual has three alleles. An individual can possess only TWO alleles of a gene at a time — one from each parent.

The ABO Blood Group System

Controlled by a gene with three alleles: IA, IB, and i.

Dominance: IA = IB > i

Type AB is special: both alleles are expressed (codominance). Type O is recessive: only expressed when two i alleles are present.

Beyond Three Alleles — Rabbit Coat Color

Rabbit coat color has four alleles with a hierarchy of dominance:

C⁺ > cch > ch > c

  • C⁺: Wild type (brown fur)
  • cch: Chinchilla (black-tipped white fur)
  • ch: Himalayan (white fur with black extremities)
  • c: Albino (white fur)

Connecting Multiple Allelism to Mendel

✅ Mendel's Principles Still Apply
  • One allele comes from each parent.
  • During gamete formation, the two alleles in an individual separate.
  • The difference is: instead of a gene having only two possible alleles in the population, there are more than two.

7 Polygenic Inheritance

🎯 Learning Targets
  • Describe polygenic inheritance.
  • Differentiate polygenic inheritance from classical Mendelian inheritance.
  • Explain how several genes contribute to one trait.
  • Predict phenotypic outcomes and ratios in a polygenic cross.

What is Polygenic Inheritance?

One character is controlled by two or more genes, with each gene contributing to the phenotype.

Classical Mendelian
  • One gene → one pair of alleles → limited phenotypes
Polygenic
  • Several genes → many allele combinations → a range of phenotypes

Why Do Polygenic Traits Show a Range?

Each gene contributes to the final phenotype. In the skin-color model:

  • More contributing dominant alleles → more melanin → darker phenotype
  • Fewer contributing dominant alleles → less melanin → lighter phenotype

Therefore, instead of only two or three possible phenotypes, many intermediate phenotypes can occur.

A Simple Model — Three Gene Pairs (AaBbCc)

3 gene pairs × 2 alleles = 6 alleles total

🔔 Bell-Shaped Distribution
The intermediate phenotype is the most common. The distribution forms a bell-shaped (normal) curve.

Polygenic vs. Mendelian Inheritance

Classical MendelianPolygenic
One gene considered for the traitSeveral genes contribute to one trait
Usually fewer phenotype categoriesMany possible phenotypes
Distinct phenotypic classesPhenotypes form a continuous range
Example: simple pea-plant traitsExamples: height, skin color
⚠️ Do Not Confuse These Extensions
  • Multiple Allelism: One gene → more than two possible alleles in the population.
  • Polygenic Inheritance: Several genes → one character.
  • Pleiotropy: One gene → affects multiple characters.
  • Epistasis: One gene affects or masks the expression of another gene.

8 Sex-Linked Inheritance

🎯 Learning Targets
  • Define sex-linked inheritance.
  • Differentiate X-linked recessive, X-linked dominant, and Y-linked inheritance.
  • Identify inheritance patterns associated with sex chromosomes.
  • Solve simple X-linked inheritance problems.
❓ Guiding Question
Why do some genetic traits show different inheritance patterns in males and females?

What is Sex-Linked Inheritance?

Occurs when a gene responsible for a trait is located on a sex chromosome.

  • Female: XX
  • Male: XY

The X chromosome contains many more genes than the Y chromosome. Therefore, many human sex-linked traits involve genes on the X chromosome.

Three Patterns of Sex-Linked Inheritance

9 Practice Problems (from the Modules)

1. A rooster with gray feathers is mated with a hen of the same phenotype. Among their offspring, 15 chicks are gray, 6 are black, and 8 are white. What is the simplest explanation for the inheritance of these colors?
Show Solution
The ratio is approximately 15:6:8, which simplifies to roughly 2:1:1 (or 1:2:1).
This is the classic 1:2:1 phenotypic ratio seen in incomplete dominance or codominance.
The gray heterozygote (e.g., BW) is intermediate (incomplete dominance) or expresses both traits (codominance) between black (BB) and white (WW).
2. Flower position, stem length, and seed shape are controlled by independently assorting genes: Axial (A) / Terminal (a), Tall (T) / Dwarf (t), Round (R) / Wrinkled (r). If a plant heterozygous for all three characters self-fertilizes, what proportion of offspring is expected to be homozygous for all three dominant traits?
Show Solution
Cross: AaTtRr × AaTtRr
Probability of AA = 1/4
Probability of TT = 1/4
Probability of RR = 1/4
Using the multiplication rule (AND): 1/4 × 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/64
3. In a monohybrid cross Gg × Gg, what is the probability of producing a gg offspring?
Show Solution
P(g from Parent 1) = 1/2
P(g from Parent 2) = 1/2
P(gg) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 (25%)
4. What is the probability of producing a heterozygous Gg offspring from a Gg × Gg cross?
Show Solution
Two mutually exclusive ways: (G from Parent 1 + g from Parent 2) OR (g from Parent 1 + G from Parent 2).
P = (1/2 × 1/2) + (1/2 × 1/2) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2 (50%)
5. A tall pea plant of unknown genotype is crossed with a dwarf plant (tt). Half the offspring are tall and half are dwarf. What was the unknown genotype?
Show Solution
The 1:1 ratio indicates the unknown parent produced two types of gametes (T and t) in equal proportion.
Therefore, the unknown parent is Tt (heterozygous tall).
6. In a dihybrid cross GgWw × GgWw, what is the probability of getting a yellow, wrinkled offspring? (Assume G=green, g=yellow, W=round, w=wrinkled).
Show Solution
Yellow (gg): 1/4 (from Gg × Gg)
Wrinkled (ww): 1/4 (from Ww × Ww)
Combined (AND rule): 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16
7. Red flower × White flower produces all Pink flowers. What pattern of inheritance is this? What would the F₂ phenotypic ratio be?
Show Solution
Pattern: Incomplete dominance — the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype.
F₁ = all RW (pink). F₂ from RW × RW = 1 RR (red) : 2 RW (pink) : 1 WW (white).
Phenotypic ratio: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white
8. A mother has Type A blood and a father has Type B blood. Their child is Type O. Determine the parents' genotypes and the possible offspring blood types.
Show Solution
Mother's genotype: IAi (must carry i to have a Type O child)
Father's genotype: IBi
Cross: IAi × IBi
Genotypic ratio: 1 IAIB : 1 IAi : 1 IBi : 1 ii
Phenotypic ratio: 1 AB : 1 A : 1 B : 1 O
Probability of Type AB child: 25%
9. In the three-gene skin color model (AaBbCc × AaBbCc), what is the most common phenotype?
Show Solution
The most common phenotype is the intermediate phenotype with 3 contributing dominant alleles (20/64 of the offspring).
The distribution forms a bell-shaped curve: 1:6:15:20:15:6:1.
10. A carrier female for an X-linked recessive condition has children with an unaffected male. What is the probability of an affected son?
Show Solution
Cross: XHXh × XHY
Sons receive Y from the father and either XH or Xh from the mother.
50% of sons receive Xh → affected son.
Probability of an affected son: 1/2 (50%)
11. An affected father has an X-linked dominant condition and the mother is unaffected. What percentage of daughters are expected to be affected?
Show Solution
Cross: XDY (affected father) × XdXd (unaffected mother)
The father gives XD to ALL daughters → 100% of daughters affected.
The father gives Y to ALL sons → 0% of sons affected.
12. A father has a Y-linked trait. Who can inherit the trait?
Show Solution
Only his sons can inherit the trait (Father → Son → Grandson).
There is no father-to-daughter transmission of a Y-linked gene.

Complete dominance vs. incomplete dominance vs. codominance — what does the heterozygote look like?
Tap to flip →
Complete: Looks like one homozygote (dominant phenotype).

Incomplete: Intermediate phenotype.

Codominance: Both traits expressed.
Compare Patterns
Which patterns give a 1:2:1 phenotypic ratio?
Tap to flip →
Incomplete dominance and codominance both give a 1:2:1 phenotypic ratio in a heterozygous × heterozygous cross.

Complete dominance gives 3:1.
Compare Patterns
Distinguish multiple allelism, polygenic inheritance, pleiotropy, and epistasis.
Tap to flip →
Multiple allelism: One gene → >2 alleles in a population.
Polygenic: Several genes → one character.
Pleiotropy: One gene → multiple characters.
Epistasis: One gene affects/masks another gene.
Problem Solving
What is the first thing you should do when solving a genetics problem?
Tap to flip →
Do not immediately construct a Punnett square. First, list all information given, identify dominant/recessive traits, assign symbols, determine parental genotypes, and determine possible gametes. Then choose the appropriate method.

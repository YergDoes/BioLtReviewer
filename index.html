Module 1

Mendelian Inheritance: From Heredity to Probability

Step 1: Core Vocabulary

  • Gene: A segment of DNA that carries instructions for a trait.
  • Allele: An alternative version of a gene (e.g., P for purple, p for white).
  • Genotype: The genetic makeup (e.g., Pp).
  • Phenotype: The observable trait (e.g., purple flowers).
  • Homozygous: Two identical alleles (PP or pp).
  • Heterozygous: Two different alleles (Pp).

Step 2: Mendel's Laws

Law of Segregation: The two alleles for a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete gets only one allele. This happens during Meiosis I.

Law of Independent Assortment: Alleles of different genes segregate independently of one another during gamete formation (e.g., seed color and seed shape).

Step 3: Probability Rules

Multiplication Rule ("AND" Rule) The probability of two independent events occurring together is the product of their individual probabilities.
Example: Probability of getting gg from Gg × Gg = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.
Addition Rule ("OR" Rule) The probability of either of two mutually exclusive events occurring is the sum of their probabilities.
Example: Probability of getting a heterozygous Gg offspring from Gg × Gg = (1/4) + (1/4) = 1/2.

Step 4: Punnett Squares & Testcrosses

Monohybrid Cross (Gg × Gg):

Gg
GGGGg
gGggg

Genotypic ratio: 1 GG : 2 Gg : 1 gg
Phenotypic ratio: 3 dominant : 1 recessive

Testcross: Cross an unknown dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive to determine the unknown genotype. If any recessive offspring appear, the unknown parent was heterozygous.

Step 5: Dihybrid Crosses & Forked-Line Method

For a dihybrid cross like GgWw × GgWw, don't draw a 16-box square. Use the forked-line method:

  • Gene 1 (Gg × Gg): 3/4 green, 1/4 yellow.
  • Gene 2 (Ww × Ww): 3/4 round, 1/4 wrinkled.
  • Multiply along branches: 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16 green, round.

Extensions of Mendelian Genetics

Step 1: Degrees of Dominance

PatternHeterozygote AppearancePhenotypic Ratio
Complete DominanceLooks like dominant homozygote3:1
Incomplete DominanceIntermediate (e.g., red + white = pink)1:2:1
CodominanceBoth traits expressed (e.g., red + white = roan)1:2:1

Step 2: Multiple Allelism

A gene has more than two alleles in a population, but an individual only inherits two.

ABO Blood Groups:

  • IA and IB are codominant.
  • i is recessive to both.
  • Type A: IAIA or IAi | Type B: IBIB or IBi | Type AB: IAIB | Type O: ii

Step 3: Polygenic Inheritance

One trait is controlled by multiple genes (e.g., skin color, height). This produces a continuous range of phenotypes, often forming a bell-shaped curve.

More dominant alleles = more extreme phenotype (e.g., darker skin).

Step 4: Sex-Linked Inheritance

  • X-linked recessive: Males are more often affected. Carrier mothers pass to affected sons. No father-to-son transmission.
  • X-linked dominant: Affected father passes to all daughters and no sons.
  • Y-linked: Only males affected. Father to son only.

Module 3

Pedigree Analysis

Step 1: Reading Pedigree Symbols

  • Square = Male
  • Circle = Female
  • Shaded = Affected individual
  • Unshaded = Unaffected individual
  • Horizontal line = Mating
  • Vertical line = Offspring

Step 2: Identifying the Inheritance Pattern

Clue in PedigreeLikely Pattern
Trait appears every generation; father-to-son transmission possible.Autosomal Dominant
Unaffected parents have an affected child; trait skips generations.Autosomal Recessive
Affected father passes to ALL daughters, no sons.X-linked Dominant
More males affected; no father-to-son transmission.X-linked Recessive
Only males affected; father passes to all sons.Y-linked

Step 3: Problem Solving Steps

  1. Determine the most likely inheritance pattern using the clues above.
  2. Assign genotypes to affected and unaffected individuals.
  3. Use Punnett squares or probability rules to calculate the chance of future offspring being affected.
Example: Two carrier parents (Aa × Aa) have a 25% chance (1/4) of having an affected child (aa).
